男生: Our first story begins in Egypt, beside the famous Sphinx. The Great Sphinx of Giza, lion-bodied and king-faced, was carved from a ridge of limestone, rock that was once a warm sea floor. Inside those rocks, scientists have recognised a crocodile never named before. The animal, Herugavialis cairensis, lived forty-five million years ago. It was a gavial, a crocodile with a long, thin snout for snapping up fish, and a curved ridge circled each of its eyes. Our hero is a skull. Collectors dug it out of the Mokattam rocks in the nineteen twenties. It travelled to a German museum and slept for almost a hundred years. Its label named a known crocodile, and nobody questioned it for decades. Then an international team took a closer look. They slid the skull into a CT scanner, a machine that lets doctors and scientists peek inside objects without breaking them open. After comparing three hundred details, the team concluded: this was not the crocodile on the label. It was a new genus, a branch that science had missed. They named it after Heru, a god linked with the Sphinx. And like the Sphinx, it is missing the tip of its snout.

男生: Our second treasure was hiding in plain sight in a university collection in Germany. It looks like a woodlouse that swallowed a growth potion: hard scaly shell, many legs. It is a giant isopod, a deep-sea cousin of the tiny pill bugs, only this one is thirty-two centimetres long. Scientists call monsters this size colossogiants. It came from the Caribbean Sea, near Puerto Rico, caught in a seabed trap five hundred metres down. Giant isopods patrol the dark ocean floor, down to two thousand five hundred metres, eating whatever sinks: fish, squid, even whales. These armoured cleaners help digest the ocean's leftovers. For forty years, its label used a cousin's name. It even starred in a sea-giants exhibition. Everyone saw it; nobody saw it. Then a researcher and the curator wheeled it into an X-ray scanner. The scans, with tiny details of mouth and shell, proved it was a new species. Its closest relative lives near Mexico's Yucatán coast. Its name joins its two homes: its birth island, and the town of its recognition. You need no ship or submarine to find a new species. Sometimes it sits on a shelf, hiding in plain sight, waiting for someone to look twice.
