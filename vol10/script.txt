男生: Splashdown! Four astronauts are home after two hundred and thirty-seven days in orbit. Their capsule, a Crew Dragon named Freedom, dropped through the sky under four parachutes and splashed into the Pacific Ocean, near California. Out came Jessica, Jack, Sophie, and Andrey — friends from three space agencies, home at last. How far did they fly? Around and around our planet: three thousand, seven hundred and ninety-two orbits, over one hundred million miles — like flying to the Moon and back two hundred times! Up there, the crew ran experiments, fixed equipment, and stepped outside on spacewalks, floating above the blue Earth. One crewmate set a record. Sophie, from France, stayed in orbit two hundred and thirty-seven days without a break, longer than any European astronaut before her. She also became the first French woman to walk in space, working outside while Earth turned below. Why stay up so long? Long missions teach doctors how bodies change without gravity: muscles, bones and eyes. Those lessons will help future explorers reach the Moon and Mars. But best of all: after two hundred and thirty-seven days, four friends are home, safe and sound, under the same sky as you.

女生: 从几百公里高的太空，我们一头扎进一座小花园。第二位主角只有你的手掌那么大，翅膀上却藏着一套连科学家都惊叹的魔法。它不会喷火，也不会隐身——它靠的是花纹。

男生: Now shrink down to the garden, where a butterfly plays a magic trick. Its wings carry stripes and bands, and when it flaps them, something strange happens to a bird's eyes. The stripes seem to slide the wrong way, so the bird misjudges where the butterfly is going, and how fast. Snap! The beak closes on empty air. Scientists in England filmed butterflies taking off with a camera a thousand times faster than a blink. They fed the films into a computer model of a bird's eyes — and saw motion that was not there. When they painted the wings plain inside the computer, the illusion vanished: the patterns were the magic. Next came three-dimensional computer butterflies — seven hundred and fifty-seven designs from nearly four hundred species — and a game: a hundred volunteers played predator, tapping at butterflies. Against the most confusing patterns, people kept striking behind the butterfly. Vertical stripes, single bands, and tails on the back wings confused the eye the most. The wings help the trick: they clap together, peel apart, and twist, so a stripe can drift down while the butterfly flies up — like the stripes on a barber's pole, climbing forever. No poison, no sting — just clever paint, perfectly timed. Few defences in nature work better.

If you spread your wings, you try new things and explore what you can do — like astronauts leaving Earth, or a butterfly on its first flight. Here's an example: "This year, I joined a new club to spread my wings." Where will your wings take you?
