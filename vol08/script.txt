男生: Meet Euclid, a space telescope from Europe. Its job is to take slow, steady photos of faraway galaxies, and to study two invisible mysteries: dark matter and dark energy. Euclid is like a careful photographer of the deep sky. It was never built to chase fast little comets.

Then, out of the blue, a streak appeared! In photos of the same patch of sky, a small smudge had moved. A young scientist in Finland, Mikko Pöntinen, had written clever software to catch moving objects — and it waved a little flag. In five sets of photos, the smudge had jumped to a new spot each time, circled by a fuzzy cloud of dust and gas. No flying saucer from a cartoon — a comet, a giant snowball of ice and rock.

But was it really new? Scientists checked every catalogue of comets and asteroids to determine the truth — no match. The world centre for small space objects confirmed it: brand new — and it now carries the telescope's name. 男生: Now let's fly back to an ancient forest in Myanmar, where dinosaurs walked. Sticky resin flowed down a tree, and one golden drop of it trapped something amazing: the tail of a small lizard — skin, muscle and tiny bones, still there ninety-nine million years later. Today that drop is hard amber, and the tail waits inside it like a message in a bottle.

But why was there only a tail? Because the lizard dropped it — on purpose! Many lizards still do this. When a hungry predator grabs the tail, the tail breaks away and keeps wiggling, distracting the hunter with a snack it can hardly digest — while the lizard dashes free! Scientists scanned the amber with special X-rays and found a neat muscle pattern inside — the same one modern lizards use to let go. Direct proof that the trick is ancient!

Still, escape is a compromise. Without a tail, a lizard climbs and balances more slowly, and a new one takes long to grow. But the maths was simple: lose a tail, keep a life! Its tail stayed in the golden amber, waiting to tell its story.
