男生: Now look up at a summer storm. A lightning bolt flashes across the sky, and a moment later comes thunder. Here is the amazing fact: the air around a lightning bolt can reach about thirty thousand degrees — about five times hotter than the surface of the Sun, which is only about five thousand five hundred degrees!

How does it happen? Inside a storm cloud, tiny ice crystals crash into each other again and again, building up electric charge, like rubbing a balloon on your hair. When the charge grows too big, it has to escape. It rushes down to the ground in a flash — and that flash is lightning.

The flash is so hot that it turns the air around it into plasma, a super-hot gas. For a tiny moment, the flash exposes the whole sky. Then the super-hot air expands fast, and that expansion is the thunder you hear. Scientists use delicate instruments to measure lightning. They say one flash lasts less than a second — but in that second, it is hotter than the Sun's surface!

Of course, the Sun's center is far hotter than any lightning bolt. But the part of the Sun we see — its surface — is cooler than a lightning flash. So next time you see lightning, remember: you are watching sky fire, hotter than sunshine itself.
